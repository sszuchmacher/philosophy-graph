In 1907 a priest who had befriended the young Martin Heidegger gave him a copy of Franz Brentano's doctoral dissertation, *On the Several Senses of Being in Aristotle*, published in 1862. Heidegger later described the book as the chief help and guide of his first clumsy attempts to find his way into philosophy. The question it posed stayed with him for the rest of his life.

Brentano's starting point was a line Aristotle repeats throughout the *Metaphysics*: being is said in many ways. We say that something *is* in the sense of the categories, as substance, quality, quantity or relation, and also in the sense of being true, being accidentally, being potentially or actually. Brentano set out to show that this multiplicity is not a chaos. All the senses of being, he argued, are ordered around one primary sense, **substance**, to which the others refer, and the categories can be derived systematically.

What Heidegger took from the book was less Brentano's solution than the question beneath it. If being has many meanings, what is the fundamental one, and what does *being* mean at all? Asking what things are presupposes some understanding of what it is to be, and Heidegger came to think the tradition since Aristotle had relied on that understanding while passing over it. *Being and Time* opens by announcing that the **question of the meaning of being** has been forgotten and must be raised anew.

There is a second line from Brentano to Heidegger, through Husserl, Brentano's student, whose phenomenology was built on Brentano's concept of **intentionality**, the directedness of consciousness toward objects. Heidegger learned phenomenology from Husserl and then turned it toward the question he had first met in Brentano's Aristotle.

A scholarly thesis on the structure of Aristotle's categories thus became the seed of one of the most ambitious ontological projects of the twentieth century. Heidegger radicalized Brentano's problem by refusing to settle it within Aristotle's framework, and asking about being itself.
