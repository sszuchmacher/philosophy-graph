The Milesian inquiry into the origin of things ran through three generations in a single city, and the third of them, Anaximenes, can be read as a partial return to the first.

Thales had named water as the principle of all things. His successor Anaximander objected, in effect, that no single visible element could be the source of the others, since each has its opposite: water would long ago have extinguished fire. He proposed instead the **apeiron**, the boundless and indefinite, without qualities of its own.

Anaximenes kept the ambition but rejected the solution. A principle with no determinate nature explains the world only by being unlike it. So he went back toward Thales' kind of answer: the principle is a definite, familiar substance, **air**, which is everywhere, invisible when evenly spread, and always in motion.

What he added was decisive. Thales, as far as our sources tell us, never said how water becomes earth or fire. Anaximenes did: through **condensation and rarefaction**. Rarefied, air becomes fire; condensed, it becomes wind, then cloud, then water, then earth, then stone. Later writers credit him with a homely test: breath blown through pursed lips feels cold, through an open mouth warm. Differences of quality are produced by differences of density.

This is the first explicit attempt to reduce qualitative change to a single quantitative process, an idea with a long future in physics. It also let Anaximenes keep Anaximander's insight that the principle must be capable of becoming everything, without giving up the Thalesian demand that it be something one can point to. Later doxographers even report that he compared the air enfolding the world to the breath, or soul, that holds each of us together.

So the lineage is not simply linear. Anaximenes answers Anaximander by partly restoring Thales, and in doing so turns Thales' bare assertion into a theory of how one thing becomes many.
