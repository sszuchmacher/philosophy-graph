Zeno defended Parmenides indirectly. Rather than prove that being is one and unchanging, he showed that the opposite assumption, that there are many things and that they move, leads to absurdity. The paradoxes of motion are the most famous of these arguments, and Aristotle, who preserves most of them, is also the philosopher who first answered them in detail.

Take the **Dichotomy**: to cross a stadium you must first reach the halfway point, then half of what remains, and so on without end; you would have to complete infinitely many tasks in a finite time. Or **Achilles**, who can never overtake the tortoise, because each time he reaches where it was, it has moved a little further. Or the **Arrow**, which at every instant occupies a space equal to itself and is therefore at rest at every instant. When, then, does it move?

Aristotle's first answer, in the *Physics*, is that time is divisible in the same way as distance: the infinitely many parts of the stadium are matched by infinitely many parts of the time, so no impossibility arises. Later in the same work he admits that this answer satisfies the questioner but not the question, and he gives a deeper one. A continuous line is **infinitely divisible potentially**, not actually. Its halfway point exists as a point only if you stop there or mark it; the runner who crosses without stopping does not actually traverse infinitely many distinct segments. The infinite is real, but only as an unending possibility of division, never as a completed collection.

Against the Arrow he denies that time is composed of indivisible instants, any more than a line is composed of points. Motion does not happen in an instant; it happens over intervals.

This distinction between **potential and actual infinity** governed thinking about the continuum until the nineteenth century, when mathematicians began to treat completed infinities as legitimate objects. Zeno's puzzles were the pressure that forced Aristotle to articulate it.
