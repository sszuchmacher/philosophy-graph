Giordano Bruno's most dangerous idea, that the universe is infinite and contains innumerable worlds, had an ancient ally. Lucretius, following Epicurus, had argued that space has no boundary: stand at the supposed edge and throw a spear, and either something stops it, which means there is more beyond, or it flies on, which means the edge is not an edge. In an unbounded space filled with endless atoms, worlds like ours must form again and again.

Bruno seized on this. In the dialogues he wrote in London in the 1580s and in the long Latin poems published in Frankfurt in 1591, he defends a universe without walls, without a centre, and without the nested crystalline spheres of Aristotelian cosmology. The stars are suns, and around them turn other earths. He even adopted Lucretius' form: his poem *On the Immense and the Innumerable* is written in Latin hexameters on the model of *On the Nature of Things*, with prose commentary alongside.

But Bruno transforms what he borrows. Lucretius' infinite is the infinite of atoms and **void**, combining by chance and watched over by no one; the gods, if they exist, take no interest. Bruno rejects the empty void. Space for him is filled with a continuous ethereal medium, and the whole is alive, animated by a **world-soul**. The infinity of the universe is not the absence of God but the only fitting expression of an infinite divine power, which could not be satisfied by producing a finite world. Here Bruno's other great source, the Neoplatonism of Plotinus, reshapes the Epicurean picture.

The result is a hybrid neither ancient school would have recognized: Epicurean cosmology inside a theology of the infinite. It was that combination, among other heresies, that the Roman Inquisition condemned when it burned Bruno in 1600.
