Erich Fromm and Herbert Marcuse began on the same side. Both were associated with the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research in the 1930s, and both tried to join Marx and Freud: to explain, through psychoanalysis, why people accept and even desire the social order that dominates them. Fromm was the Institute's leading psychoanalyst, central to its studies of authority and the family. By the end of the decade, however, he had broken with Horkheimer and Adorno, largely over his revision of Freud, and left.

That revision is what Marcuse attacked. In the epilogue to *Eros and Civilization* (1955), titled *Critique of Neo-Freudian Revisionism*, he took aim at the school of Fromm, Karen Horney and Harry Stack Sullivan. They had played down Freud's theory of **instincts**, the sexual drive, the death drive and the repression civilization demands, and emphasized instead culture, character and interpersonal relations. For Fromm, the goal of a good society was the flourishing of a loving, **productive personality**.

To Marcuse this looked like a retreat dressed as progress. Freud's instinct theory, however dated its biology, contained a radical truth: civilization is built on the repression of desire, and existing society demands more of it than necessary, what Marcuse called **surplus repression**. By dissolving the instincts into social relations and moral ideals, the revisionists lost the critical edge. Their ethics of love and productiveness, preached within an unfree society, became an ideology of adjustment: advice on living well in conditions that ought to be changed.

Fromm replied in the journal *Dissent* that Marcuse had misread him, and that Marcuse's own hopes for the liberation of instinct led toward nihilism. The exchange grew bitter and was never resolved.

The dispute defined two directions for the meeting of Marx and Freud: Fromm's humanist psychology of character, widely read by a general public, and Marcuse's instinct-based utopianism, which would inspire the student movements of the 1960s.
