In *Process and Reality* (1929), Alfred North Whitehead wrote one of the most quoted sentences in modern philosophy:

> The safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato.

He meant it less as a quip than as a programme. His own metaphysics was an attempt to rethink Plato's questions in the light of modern physics.

Whitehead's basic realities are not enduring substances but **actual occasions**: momentary events of experience that arise, take up their past, and perish. A world of pure process needs something to account for its definiteness, for why an occasion becomes this rather than something else. Here Whitehead reintroduces a Platonic element. **Eternal objects**, pure potentials such as particular shades of colour, shapes and relations, are the forms of definiteness that actual occasions can realize. Like Plato's Forms they are timeless and can be shared by many things. Unlike them, they are not a higher reality that the world imperfectly copies. They exist only as possibilities, and become real only by entering the process of becoming, what Whitehead called their **ingression**.

Whitehead's favourite dialogue was not the *Republic* but the *Timaeus*, Plato's cosmology, which he admired for presenting the world as the work of a craftsman bringing order to a recalcitrant medium. In *Adventures of Ideas* he singles out Plato's conviction that the creation of the world is the victory of **persuasion over force**. His own God is not an omnipotent creator but a lure: the source of the possibilities each occasion may take up, working by attraction rather than compulsion.

This is Plato read backward through relativity and evolution: Forms without a separate heaven, a cosmos still in the making. Whitehead's footnote to Plato is that the Forms themselves exist for the sake of becoming.
