Heraclitus rarely argues with his predecessors; he passes sentence on them. Among the targets of his most famous verdict is Pythagoras:

> Much learning does not teach understanding; otherwise it would have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, and also Xenophanes and Hecataeus.

In another fragment he goes further, accusing Pythagoras of having pursued inquiry more than anyone and then fashioning from others' writings a wisdom of his own: **polymathy** and artful fraud.

The charge is not that Pythagoras knew too little but that he knew in the wrong way. The Pythagorean way of life gathered a great deal: number, harmony, astronomy, rules of purity, doctrines of the soul's wandering through many bodies. It was transmitted within a community, partly as secret teaching, and its authority rested heavily on the master himself. For Heraclitus, wisdom is none of this. It is one thing: to understand the **logos**, the account according to which all things happen. And this logos is common, open to anyone awake enough to listen, not reserved for initiates.

There is more agreement beneath the hostility than Heraclitus admits. Both look for a hidden order behind appearances, and both describe that order as a kind of **harmony**: the Pythagoreans through the ratios of musical intervals, Heraclitus through the fitting-together of opposites in tension, like the bow and the lyre. Where they part is in what that harmony is. For the Pythagoreans it is a stable mathematical structure that the purified soul can contemplate. For Heraclitus it is strife itself, a balance that exists only in continual conflict and change.

The quarrel sets a pattern that runs through the rest of Greek philosophy: is wisdom a body of accumulated knowledge, or a single insight into how things hang together? Heraclitus stakes out the second answer, and he does so by making Pythagoras the emblem of the first.
