In the summer of 1749, Denis Diderot was imprisoned at Vincennes for his *Letter on the Blind*, and his friend Jean-Jacques Rousseau walked out from Paris to visit him. On the way, Rousseau read in a periodical the question set by the Academy of Dijon for its prize essay: had the restoration of the sciences and arts contributed to purifying morals? By his own later account he was overwhelmed on the roadside by a flood of ideas. He answered no, and won.

The *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts* was aimed, in effect, at the project Diderot was leading. The *Encyclopédie* was the Enlightenment's great wager: that gathering, ordering and spreading knowledge of every art and science would make people freer, more capable and better. Rousseau argued the reverse. The progress of knowledge had gone together with luxury, vanity, idleness and the decline of **civic virtue**; polish had replaced honesty, and learning served to adorn people's chains.

At first the opposition stayed within the friendship. Diderot encouraged the essay, and would later claim to have suggested its direction, and Rousseau wrote articles on music and on political economy for the *Encyclopédie* itself. But the difference deepened. For Diderot, sociability and knowledge were humanity's hope; for Rousseau, society as it existed was the source of human corruption, and the self could be recovered only by withdrawal and independence.

The break came in 1757 and 1758. Rousseau took personally a line from one of Diderot's plays, that only the wicked man lives alone, and the quarrel spread through their circle. Soon after, Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert* publicly attacked the proposal, made in an *Encyclopédie* article by its co-editor, to establish a theatre in Geneva.

The rupture is more than a personal story. Rousseau's critique of **progress** from within the Enlightenment set the pattern for later critics, Romantic, conservative and radical alike, who would ask whether civilization's advances make people happier or freer at all.
