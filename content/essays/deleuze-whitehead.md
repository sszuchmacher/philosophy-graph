Gilles Deleuze had a taste for philosophers outside the mainstream, and in Whitehead he found an ally for his own project: a philosophy in which **becoming**, not being, comes first. He devoted a chapter of *The Fold: Leibniz and the Baroque* (1988) to the question that organizes Whitehead's metaphysics: what is an event?

For Whitehead the world is made not of enduring things but of events. Even the Great Pyramid, he had written, is an event, a continuous happening that only looks like a static object. Deleuze reconstructs the conditions under which an event can emerge from chaos. There must be **extension**, a series of parts organized like the harmonics of a vibration; **intensity**, the intrinsic qualities that give the series its properties; and **individuals**, which Whitehead analyses as prehensions, each new occasion grasping, feeling and integrating the occasions before it.

The comparison with Leibniz drives the chapter. Leibniz's monads each express the whole world from a point of view, but they are closed, without windows, and a pre-established harmony guarantees that their worlds fit together in the best possible way. Whitehead's occasions are open: each directly takes in its predecessors and passes itself on to its successors. Deleuze reads this as the moment when philosophy moves from a world that converges on a single best order to a world of divergent series, bifurcation and novelty, what he calls, borrowing Joyce's word, a **chaosmos**.

Deleuze did not become a Whiteheadian. But he took from Whitehead a vocabulary and a confidence: that metaphysics can be rebuilt around events, that concepts should serve the creation of the new, and that a philosophy of process need not dissolve into vagueness. The encounter also helped bring a largely neglected Anglo-American metaphysician back into the centre of continental philosophy.
