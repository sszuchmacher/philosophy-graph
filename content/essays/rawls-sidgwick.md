Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874) is the most careful work of classical utilitarianism. It does not simply assert that right action maximizes happiness; it compares the main methods of moral reasoning, egoism, intuitionism and utilitarianism, with extraordinary patience, and concludes that the utilitarian principle best systematizes common-sense morality. Its conclusion is famously troubled: Sidgwick could not show why a rational agent should prefer the general good to their own, and he called this the **dualism of practical reason**.

When John Rawls set out, nearly a century later, to offer a systematic alternative to utilitarianism, it was Sidgwick's version he took as the classical doctrine to be answered. In *A Theory of Justice* he treats Sidgwick's formulation as its clearest statement, and he later wrote the foreword to a new edition of the *Methods*.

The heart of Rawls's objection targets a move Sidgwick made explicit: judging from the **point of view of the universe**, where one person's good counts for no more than anyone else's. Extended from one life to a whole society, this impartiality treats the gains and losses of different people as if they could be summed and traded, like the satisfactions of a single person over time. The sacrifices of some can then be justified by larger benefits to others. Utilitarianism, Rawls concludes, **does not take seriously the distinction between persons**.

His alternative, principles chosen behind a veil of ignorance that secure equal basic liberties and arrange inequalities to benefit the least advantaged, is designed to block exactly those trade-offs.

Yet the debt runs deeper than opposition. Sidgwick's practice of testing principles against considered moral judgements, and revising each in the light of the other, anticipates the procedure Rawls called **reflective equilibrium**, and Rawls held Sidgwick up as a model of how to do moral philosophy systematically. The critique of utilitarianism was conducted, fittingly, in Sidgwick's own spirit.
