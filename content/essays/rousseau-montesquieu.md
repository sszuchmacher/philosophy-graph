Montesquieu's *The Spirit of the Laws*, published in 1748, was the most admired political book of the French Enlightenment. Rousseau read it closely, cited it with respect, and built part of *The Social Contract* on its foundations, and against its conclusions.

What Rousseau accepted was Montesquieu's **political sociology**. Laws are not abstract commands; they must suit the physical and moral situation of a people: its climate, the fertility of its soil, its size, its customs. Rousseau says so explicitly in a chapter on why not every form of government suits every country:

> Liberty, not being a fruit of all climates, is not within the reach of all peoples.

The more one considers this principle laid down by Montesquieu, he adds, the more one feels its truth. His later advice to the legislators of Corsica and Poland would be shaped by the same attention to circumstance.

What he rejected was Montesquieu's model of freedom. Montesquieu had found liberty in the English constitution, where legislative, executive and judicial powers are separated and check one another, while intermediate bodies stand between the monarch and the people. Freedom, on this view, is security under a balanced structure.

For Rousseau such a structure mistakes the nature of sovereignty. The sovereign is the **general will** of the people as a whole, and a will cannot be divided without being destroyed; those who carve sovereignty into powers, he writes, make of it a fantastic creature pieced together from parts. Nor can it be **represented**. The English, he famously remarks, think themselves free, but are free only while electing members of Parliament; once they are elected, the people are enslaved.

The two thinkers mark a fork in modern political thought. Montesquieu's line runs toward constitutionalism, checks and balances and pluralism; Rousseau's toward popular sovereignty and self-government. Both look beyond abstract principle to the conditions of actual peoples. They disagree about where freedom lives.
