Hannah Arendt came to Heidelberg in 1926 to study with Karl Jaspers, who supervised her dissertation on the concept of love in Augustine. The relationship that began there lasted until Jaspers's death in 1969, through her flight from Germany, internment, exile and fame, and it is recorded in one of the great philosophical correspondences of the century.

Jaspers's existential philosophy centred on the individual confronting **limit situations**, such as death, suffering, struggle and guilt, in which ordinary certainties fail. But unlike many existentialists, he insisted that truth is not reached in solitude. It is found in **communication**, a loving struggle between people who remain open to one another. Philosophy, for Jaspers, is essentially dialogue, and reason is the will to communicate without limit.

Arendt transformed this idea into a political one. Her insistence on **plurality**, that human beings exist in the plural, that the world appears differently from each standpoint, and that political judgement depends on thinking from the standpoints of others, gives Jaspers's ethics of communication a public setting. Where Heidegger, her other teacher, offered an analysis of the solitary self facing its own existence, Jaspers offered a model of thinking that already took place between people.

The connection became concrete after the war. In *The Question of German Guilt* (1946), Jaspers distinguished criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt, insisting that citizens bear political responsibility for their state's acts even when they are not personally criminals. Arendt's later reflections on responsibility, collective guilt and judgement, above all in the controversy over *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, when Jaspers stood by her, developed within this shared framework, even where she disagreed with him.

In her tribute *Karl Jaspers: Citizen of the World?* she saw in him the rare figure of a philosopher whose thinking was oriented toward humanity as a whole. Jaspers, as much as any teacher, showed her that philosophy and politics could meet.
