Marx famously claimed to have found Hegel's dialectic standing on its head and to have set it on its feet. It was Engels, though, who spelled out what a materialist dialectic was supposed to be, and he did so in a way that went well beyond anything Marx wrote.

In *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* (1886), Engels drew a distinction that became standard. Hegel's philosophy contains a **system** and a **method**. The system is conservative: it culminates in absolute knowledge and, politically, in something close to the Prussian state as the realization of reason. The method is revolutionary: it holds that nothing is final, that every form contains the seeds of its own dissolution. Keep the method, discard the system, and replace Hegel's Idea with matter in motion.

In *Anti-Dühring* and in the unfinished manuscripts published after his death as *Dialectics of Nature*, Engels applied that method not only to history and society but to **nature itself**. From Hegel's logic he abstracted general laws of dialectics: the transformation of quantity into quality, as water turns to steam at a certain temperature; the interpenetration of opposites; and the negation of the negation, illustrated by a grain of barley that perishes in producing a plant, which in turn yields many grains. These laws, he argued, are at work in physics, chemistry and biology as much as in human history, and the sciences of his time, from thermodynamics to cell theory and Darwin's theory of evolution, confirmed them.

This extension turned Marxism into a complete worldview, a philosophy of nature as well as of history, and later Soviet orthodoxy built its **dialectical materialism** on it. It has been contested ever since. Critics within Marxism argued that dialectics belongs to the relation between subject and object in history and makes little sense applied to inert nature.

Either way, Engels's reading shaped how generations of Marxists received Hegel: as a method to be naturalized.
