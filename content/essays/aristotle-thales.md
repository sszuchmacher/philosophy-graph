Thales wrote nothing that survives. What we call his philosophy is, to a remarkable degree, a portrait painted by Aristotle some two and a half centuries later, and Aristotle painted it for a purpose.

In the first book of the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle surveys his predecessors to show that they were groping toward his own doctrine of the **four causes**. The earliest thinkers, he says, recognized only the material cause: the stuff out of which things come and into which they return. At the head of this line he places Thales:

> Thales, the founder of this type of philosophy, says the principle is water.

Aristotle is careful to mark the limits of what he knows. He does not report Thales' reasons; he conjectures them. Perhaps Thales noticed that nourishment is moist, that heat itself is generated from moisture, that the seeds of all things have a moist nature. Elsewhere, in *On the Soul*, he adds two stray remarks attributed to Thales: that the magnet has a soul because it moves iron, and that all things are full of gods.

What makes this a genuine turning point is not the answer but the form of the question. Instead of genealogies of gods, Thales, on Aristotle's telling, asked what single, persisting nature underlies the changing world, and answered with something one can observe. That is the move Aristotle recognizes as the birth of **natural philosophy**.

But the recognition is also a reinterpretation. Aristotle reads Thales' water as an *archê* in his own technical sense: a substrate that persists through change while its qualities alter. Whether Thales thought in those terms is doubtful; the vocabulary of substrate and attribute is Aristotle's. The founder of philosophy enters history already translated, as the first step on a staircase that conveniently leads to the *Metaphysics*.

The debt runs both ways. Thales gave Aristotle a beginning for his story of causes; Aristotle gave Thales the only lasting voice he has.
