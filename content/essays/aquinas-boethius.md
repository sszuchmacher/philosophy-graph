Boethius stands between two worlds. Educated in the Greek philosophy of late antiquity and writing in Latin under Gothic rule, he set out to transmit Plato and Aristotle to the Latin West. Beyond his logical translations he left a handful of short theological treatises and, written in prison before his execution, the *Consolation of Philosophy*. Thomas Aquinas, seven centuries later, treated these works as authorities and took from them definitions that became fixtures of scholastic thought.

Aquinas wrote commentaries on two of Boethius's treatises. In the one on *On the Trinity* he uses Boethius's division of speculative knowledge into natural philosophy, mathematics and theology, distinguished by how far their objects are removed from matter and motion, to work out how philosophical and revealed theology relate.

Two of Boethius's definitions pass almost intact into the *Summa Theologiae*. The first concerns **eternity**, which the *Consolation* defines as the whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of endless life. Eternity is not a very long time; it is life without succession, possessed all at once. Aquinas adopts the definition and defends it point by point. The second concerns **person**: an individual substance of a rational nature, a formula Boethius coined in a treatise against Christological heresies, which Aquinas applies to human beings and, with careful qualifications, to God.

The *Consolation* also supplied the classic solution to a problem Aquinas had to face: how divine foreknowledge can coexist with human freedom. Because God is eternal, Boethius argued, God does not foresee future events as a spectator looks ahead; God sees all times as present, as we may watch someone walking without making them walk. Aquinas follows this reasoning closely.

The line from Boethius to Aquinas is less a debate than a **transmission**: the late-antique synthesis of Plato and Aristotle, preserved in Latin, becoming the vocabulary of high scholasticism.
